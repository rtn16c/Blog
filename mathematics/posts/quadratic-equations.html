Quadratics 1: Expanding, Factorising, and Solving in Standard Form

20 October 2026
algebra quadratics factorising polynomials

What is a Quadratic Equation?

Simply put, a quadratic equation is one where \(x^2\) is the highest power of \(x\). Equations where the highest power is just \(x\) (i.e. \(x^1\)) are linear. Equations where the highest power is \(x^3\) are cubic. Equations with even higher powers are all forms of polynomial expressions — a quadratic is a polynomial, but not all polynomials are quadratic.

Standard Form

The standard form of a quadratic equation is:

$$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$$

Where \(a \neq 0\) — if \(a\) were zero, the \(ax^2\) term would vanish and we would just have \(bx + c = 0\), which is a linear equation, not a quadratic.

In standard form:

  • \(a\) is the coefficient of \(x^2\)
  • \(b\) is the coefficient of \(x\)
  • \(c\) is the constant

For a concrete example, take:

$$x^2 + 5x + 6 = 0$$

Here \(a = 1\), \(b = 5\), and \(c = 6\).

Remember: Just because you can't see a number doesn't mean it isn't there. All three of these are still quadratic equations:
  • \(x^2 - 12 = 0\)
  • \(x^2 + 5x = 0\)
  • \(x^2 = 0\)
Writing in the hidden zero terms to match standard form more closely:
  • \(x^2 + 0x - 12 = 0\)
  • \(x^2 + 5x + 0 = 0\)
  • \(x^2 + 0x + 0 = 0\)

Rearranging into Standard Form

A quadratic equation isn't always handed to you in standard form — you may need to rearrange it first. For example:

$$x^2 + 5x = 6$$

Whatever we do to one side of an equation we must do to the other to keep it balanced. Subtracting 6 from both sides gives:

$$x^2 + 5x - 6 = 0$$

Now it is in standard form and ready to work with.

Expanding

Quadratic expressions can appear in different forms — one common form is two sets of brackets multiplied together, such as:

$$(x + 2)(x + 3)$$

To expand this, every term in the first bracket must be multiplied by every term in the second bracket. A handy mnemonic for two-term brackets is FOIL:

  • First — the first terms of each bracket: \(x \cdot x = x^2\)
  • Outside — the outermost terms of the whole expression: \(x \cdot 3 = 3x\)
  • Inside — the innermost terms of the whole expression: \(2 \cdot x = 2x\)
  • Last — the last terms of each bracket: \(2 \cdot 3 = 6\)
Note: FOIL only works for expressions that are two sets of brackets with two terms each. With more brackets the approach is the same in principle — each term in each bracket must be multiplied by each term in every other bracket — but you'll need to be more systematic.

Putting the FOIL results together:

$$x^2 + 3x + 2x + 6$$

The two middle terms both contain \(x\) to the same power, so we can collect them:

$$x^2 + 5x + 6$$

Factorising

Factorising is the reverse of expanding. We start with a standard-form expression and want to write it as a product of two brackets. This makes solving the equation much easier (covered in the next section).

The process in six steps:

  1. Multiply \(a\) and \(c\)
  2. Find two numbers that add to \(b\) and multiply to \(a \times c\)
  3. Split the middle term using those two numbers
  4. Group the four terms into pairs
  5. Factorise each pair
  6. Factor out the common bracket

Example 1 — coefficient of \(x^2\) is 1

$$x^2 + 13x + 40$$

Here \(a = 1\), so \(a \times c = 1 \times 40 = 40\). We need two numbers that add to \(13\) and multiply to \(40\). That's 5 and 8.

Split the middle term:

$$x^2 + 8x + 5x + 40$$

Group into pairs:

$$(x^2 + 8x) + (5x + 40)$$

Factorise each group — the first pair shares \(x\), the second shares \(5\):

$$x(x + 8) + 5(x + 8)$$

Both terms now contain \((x + 8)\), so factor that out:

$$(x + 5)(x + 8)$$

Example 2 — coefficient of \(x^2\) is not 1

$$3x^2 + 11x + 6$$

Here \(a = 3\), so \(a \times c = 3 \times 6 = 18\). We need two numbers that add to \(11\) and multiply to \(18\). That's 9 and 2.

Split the middle term:

$$3x^2 + 9x + 2x + 6$$

Group into pairs:

$$(3x^2 + 9x) + (2x + 6)$$

Factorise each group — the first shares \(3x\), the second shares \(2\):

$$3x(x + 3) + 2(x + 3)$$
Remember: If the brackets don't match at this point, check your signs and your common factors. Sometimes you need to take out a negative to make them match.

Both terms contain \((x + 3)\), so factor that out:

$$(3x + 2)(x + 3)$$

Solving

Recall that the standard form is \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\), so our factorised equation is:

$$(3x + 2)(x + 3) = 0$$

For this product to equal zero, at least one of the two brackets must be zero. We can therefore solve each bracket independently:

$$3x + 2 = 0 \implies 3x = -2 \implies x = -\tfrac{2}{3}$$ $$x + 3 = 0 \implies x = -3$$

The solution is \(x = -\dfrac{2}{3}\) or \(x = -3\).
